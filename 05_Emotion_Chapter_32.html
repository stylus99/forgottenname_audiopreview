Chapter 32 The city conservators would not let Corren touch the copper sleeve. They worked beneath a canvas roof beside the parcel depot while rain lifted grit from the paving. The culvert objects remained in shallow trays packed with their own silt. Sere stood outside the marked line. Talin's red observer cord kept her beside Sere. Corren's compromised seal admitted no one. The senior conservator opened the sleeve with wooden wedges. The inward fold held a second layer against the corroded wall: a narrow strip of mineral paper, far younger than the woven fiber recovered beneath the city. It bore a custody number from the restricted comparison register Corren had defended. Corren asked the conservator to stop. “On whose authority?” Sere said. “The number identifies a protected person.” “It identifies a record.” “The distinction may not survive opening.” City custody required preservation of the object. Federation privacy required protection of living subjects. Neither rule gave Corren control. Talin said, “Read only the handling fields. No designation, location, or relationship description.” The fields had been added fourteen years earlier, when the sleeve was found during a drain repair and briefly entered Federation custody. One entry recorded transfer to a restricted surviving anchor under paired seal. A later entry recorded contact requested by I.V., professional examiner. It required a continuity witness. The witness field carried Sere's professional number. Her license had been active but junior then. She remembered no request, no sleeve, no restricted person. Corren's countersignature appeared beneath the risk review. Sere read the date twice. “You knew before Ilyra died.” “I knew she requested contact.” “You knew she named me.” “As continuity witness.” “You knew what that function meant.” Corren's attention went to the conservator's hands. “I knew what Ilyra believed it meant.” The answer was narrower than denial. Recognition came first, unwelcome and exact. Corren had not merely encountered Sere's number in the present inquiry; she had known its older significance well enough to measure risk around it. The recognition should have restored a missing edge to Sere's history. Instead it showed how long that edge had been held beyond her reach. Sere felt the old professional impulse to reward precision. Corren had answered only what the field established. That discipline was one of the reasons Sere had trusted her. Now the same discipline outlined the betrayal without softening it. Corren could keep every sentence accurate and still have built fourteen years in which Sere was the least informed person in decisions about Sere. The handling strip showed that the meeting occurred. Ilyra entered under one seal. A second person entered under witness status; the number had been abraded until only its final two digits remained. They matched Sere's. Abrasion was not proof. The named request was. The final field recorded comparison consequence observed and immediate separation of all three participants. Corren had signed that field too. “What consequence?” Sere asked. “The field cannot be read without the protected annex.” “Can you read the annex?” Corren waited one beat too long. “I read it then.” Rain hammered the canvas. A conservator moved a bucket beneath a seam. The work continued around the sentence because old material could not wait for private injury. “When did you first learn I might be at risk?” Sere asked. “That day.” “Before or after the meeting?” “After.” “When did you tell me?” Corren did not answer. The hurt found structure. “When I reopened Ilyra's case, did you know the relationship between us was operationally relevant?” “Yes.” “When you selected the incidents in the restricted comparison register?” “Yes.” “When you watched me connect sites through practices absent from my record?” “Yes.” “When I surrendered examiner standing without knowing why the channel treated me as a subject?” Corren looked at her. “Yes.” Talin asked the conservator to seal the handling strip under city custody and record Corren as a prior signatory, not present authority. The change fit one line. Corren no longer controlled the oldest evidence she recognized. The conservator asked who should receive notice when the strip entered the city catalog. Ordinary rules notified every prior signatory. That would alert the restricted office whose current route remained unnamed. Sere could omit Corren and keep the discovery quiet. The omission would repeat the act she was exposing: deciding another person's relevant knowledge was too dangerous to permit. “Notify her,” Sere said. “And notify Talin.” “Observer Senn has no evidence standing.” “Then notify her as a named prior custodian.” The city could accept that designation only if Corren certified it. Corren signed without asking to recover authority. The conservators lifted the woven strip into a humidity box. A junior worker called measurements while another recorded them. Their calm voices kept the old material separate from the breach beside it. “Did you know the sleeve was here?” Sere asked. “No. I knew a restricted object had been returned to place after the meeting. The annex did not identify which object or place.” “Did you search?” “I was prohibited from reconstructing the route.” “And you obeyed.” “I believed the prohibition protected the anchor.” The answer limited Corren's knowledge. It also showed how readily she had accepted an authority that denied Sere the same choice. Rainwater crossed the paving toward the open culvert. The workers stopped to build a ridge of sandbags. Sere joined them with her injured hand wrapped in dry cloth. Corren took the opposite end of a heavy bag. They carried it together because water did not honor broken trust. At the culvert mouth, Sere released first. * * * They crossed the market because Sere would not enter the Federation interview room under Corren's sponsorship. Morning vendors replaced rain-spoiled labels. Talin bought a paper cone of onions, handed Sere one, realized what she had done, and took it back. Neither laughed. They stopped beneath the tram awning. Corren remained an arm's length away, outside the space Talin ordinarily left for a colleague. “Tell me what you knew,” Sere said. “The restricted person had remained stable through separated custody, place-bound practices, and limited contact. Ilyra believed direct relational continuity could repair a gap in one of those supports.” “Did it?” “Something changed after the meeting.” “What?” “The anchor anticipated a route that had not been disclosed. You recognized a handling rule absent from every surviving placement record. Ilyra reported emotional certainty without event recall.” No scene returned. No voice. The absence stayed absent. “And you separated us.” “I ordered separation of source material and participants. The restricted person stabilized. Your anticipations stopped being documented.” “Because they stopped?” “Because Ilyra removed you from the reporting channel.” The distinction struck harder than accusation. “You knew she continued working with me.” “I knew she continued assigning you authority. I did not know every contact.” “Did you warn her?” “Repeatedly.” “Did you warn me?” “No.” A tram arrived. People crowded beneath the awning. Sere stepped away from Corren rather than let ordinary pressure close the distance between them. “Why?” Talin asked. “Because directed attention changed outcomes in cases I had seen. People told they might hold continuity began testing themselves and those around them. Comparison sometimes spread anticipations across separated records and practices. I believed describing the risk to Sere could increase it.” “Believed,” Sere said. “Yes.” “Evidence?” “Incomplete cases. Biased selection. Some ordinary suggestion. Some changes I could not explain.” Corren did not inflate her certainty to defend herself. It made the breach harder. “You decided uncertainty gave you authority over mine.” “I decided an undisclosed hazard justified limiting disclosure.” “About my work?” “About your exposure.” “About me.” Corren's silence conceded the center. Anger arrived without choosing a clean object. It fixed first on Corren, whose caution had become possession. Then it turned toward Ilyra, who had apparently treated Sere's authority as transferable into a danger Sere could not now remember accepting. Then it found Sere herself: the earlier Sere who might have agreed, might have trusted, might have asked the wrong narrow question and called the answer sufficient. She could prosecute none of those possibilities. Corren's silence established no motive beyond the limits of the record. Ilyra's planning established neither deception nor consent. The blank in Sere's memory established only that she could not recover the terms by which other people still claimed to have acted. Hurt supplied defendants faster than evidence supplied charges. Ilyra had placed authority in Sere's hands, perhaps without enough explanation. Corren had removed information from them. One had risked dependence. The other had enforced ignorance. Protection complicated both and absolved neither. “What consequence did your silence have?” Sere asked. “You continued linked-site comparison without knowing it might affect you. You treated the apprenticeship gap as evidence to solve rather than exposure to manage. You consented to restrictions without the history required to judge them. And you trusted my recommendations as if I had disclosed my material interests.” The confession arrived only after the record made it unavoidable. “Material interests?” Talin said. “I signed the old separation. If the risk model was wrong, my restrictions helped erase Sere's access to her own history. If it was right, my later silence may still have increased the harm by leaving Ilyra to manage it alone.” “You do not get to call the choice necessary,” Sere said. “You can call it yours.” Corren accepted the sentence. * * * At Talin's review room, the physical key no longer admitted Corren. Sere had changed the evidence designation from sponsored medical review to subject-held continuity inquiry. The change ended Corren's formal access and the exception that allowed Talin to act under her seal. “My observer standing terminates with sponsorship,” Talin said. “I know.” “You will have no counsel inside the room.” “I know.” “This costs you the only review route currently open.” Sere signed. The deeper answer had taken Corren's authority and Talin's formal presence in the same act. They reconstructed no memory. They compared preserved actions. Fourteen years earlier, Ilyra requested contact with a restricted surviving anchor and named Sere as the witness whose continuity mattered. Corren reviewed the meeting and documented destabilization risk. Afterward she ordered separation. In the present inquiry, she recognized Sere's network function, selected comparison cases, anticipated linked-site risk, and withheld the earlier exposure. One contradiction remained. Corren had told the licensing panel that her register contained cases in which comparison plausibly altered the evidence environment. The old meeting qualified, yet its aggregate had been excluded. “Why?” Sere asked through the open door. Corren stood in the corridor and did not cross the boundary. “Ilyra challenged the classification. She argued the meeting exposed a preexisting relation rather than creating a new effect. The panel sealed the case as unresolved.” “You selected the denominator.” “Yes.” “You omitted the case that most directly concerned me.” “Yes.” “Because disclosure might change me.” “And because including a disputed case as evidence of hazard would have been methodologically false.” Two reasons. One defensible. One unreviewable. The breach lived where Corren had made both decisions alone. Talin placed the old incident form on the threshold. “Did you know Sere was part of Ilyra's anchor network?” “I knew Ilyra treated her as operationally necessary across separated supports.” “Did you know instability could move toward her?” “I knew the meeting created that risk. I did not know whether it had.” “What did I know when I entered?” Sere asked. “The request described a continuity consultation. Ilyra said you had agreed to witness disputed handling.” “Did you verify my agreement?” “No.” “Why?” “Ilyra held your reciprocal professional authorization. At the time, I treated that as sufficient.” “For ordinary custody.” “Yes.” “This was not ordinary custody.” “No.” Corren had relied on Ilyra where risk required her to ask Sere. Later she relied on risk where autonomy required her to tell Sere. In both directions, someone else had decided what Sere's consent could carry. What she had lost was not only memory of an event. She had lost custody of the person whose choices still constrained the present. If that earlier woman had consented, Sere could not ask what she had understood, how long she had agreed to be bound, or what condition would have ended the agreement. If she had refused, Sere could not produce the refusal. Either possibility existed now as material other people could invoke and she could not examine from inside her own life. The unfairness tempted her toward an easy correction: treat every undocumented choice as coercion, every silence as proof of concealment, every later protection as evidence that Ilyra had known the original act was wrong. The correction would return moral custody by inventing evidentiary custody. Sere recognized the bargain because institutions made it whenever a clean finding mattered more than a damaged source. She let the anger remain divided. Corren had withheld. Ilyra had arranged. Sere might once have agreed. The system had allowed reciprocal professional authority to stand in for a present question. Those facts and possibilities did not cancel one another, and none authorized her to manufacture the missing answer. The protected strip humiliated Sere more than an accusation would have. Other people possessed the route by which she had once known Ilyra, while Sere held only its consequences. Anger moved toward Corren, then toward Ilyra, then back toward the woman Sere had been and could not question. She could not know whether she had consented to controlled contact, to reciprocal risk, or to the later separation. A missing consent record did not prove refusal. A surviving authority did not bind the present. Corren declined to characterize what Sere and Ilyra had been to one another. Sere wanted the secrecy itself to become proof. She stopped. “Record the refusal.” Talin recorded it without motive. Sere's pain remained real and non-evidentiary. Talin asked whether Corren spoke with Sere after the separation. “I conducted a routine exposure interview under a general placement review.” “You questioned her without disclosing the event being assessed.” “Yes.” Sere remembered no interview. She remembered a season of forms appearing without cases attached and irritation at being asked whether familiar corridors felt altered. The emotional fit returned without a scene. She did not promote it to memory. “What did I answer?” “No undisclosed anticipation. No unexplained recognition. You reported dreams about unfinished work but refused to classify them as evidence.” That sounded like Sere. It did not give the interview back to her. The likeness was intimate in the worst way. Corren possessed a sentence shaped like Sere's mind, dated to a season Sere could not enter. For an instant she wanted to claim it as continuity: proof that the woman in the forms was still here. Then she wanted to reject it because Corren had preserved it. Neither desire altered custody. A recognizable method was not a recovered memory, and an adversarial custodian did not make an authentic record false. “And you concluded?” “No active effect established. Continued observation without direct prompts.” “How long?” “Six months.” “You did not merely withhold a warning,” Sere said. “You converted my ordinary life into a risk record.” “Yes.” “Did Ilyra know?” “She discovered it in the fourth month and ended my access.” The old objection had been more than methodological dispute. Ilyra had stopped Corren from watching Sere without consent. Sere gained one fact about Ilyra and lost the ability to receive it cleanly. Ilyra had protected her after helping place her at risk. Betrayal and protection occupied the same act without resolving each other. Sere could not turn Ilyra's intervention into absolution; four months of observation had already happened. She could not turn the earlier risk into proof that Ilyra had knowingly sacrificed her. Other people held both pieces. Sere received them as findings about her own past and still lacked standing to question the woman at their center. Method returned because nothing else could give the loss a boundary. Sere separated the authenticated objection, Corren's account of its timing, and the meaning she wanted to assign. She could certify that Ilyra ended access. She could infer that consent mattered to the objection. She could not certify why Ilyra waited, what she had known at the meeting, or what she would have called the relation between them. “Did you withhold that material truth while she made decisions about contact, comparison, and standing?” “Yes.” Sere removed Corren's risk seal from the active file. The wax broke cleanly. She placed both pieces in a plain envelope and returned it across the threshold. “You may submit evidence. You may not classify my access to it.” “May I continue medical monitoring if you request it?” Corren asked. The careful conditional arrived too late to be neutral. “No.” The answer cost Sere the only practitioner in reach who understood restricted-anchor risk. She accepted the exposure rather than let necessity erase the boundary she had made. Talin wrote the refusal into the open record. “Sere declines Corren's monitoring. She does not decline care.” “I did not ask you to qualify it.” “The institution will otherwise record that you refused all care.” Sere read the sentence. Talin was protecting process, not interpreting feeling. “Keep it.” They requested ordinary baseline care from a city physician with no restricted-case authority. Until the appointment, Sere would hold her symptom log under dual custody with Talin, who could witness its existence but not diagnose it. Corren supplied the name of an independent specialist and did not contact her. Every useful act now had to pass through the breach without pretending the breach was gone. Ending sponsorship also reopened the licensing panel's restrictions. Without a recognized risk examiner, Sere could not enter the parcel depot, receive restricted extracts, or challenge the current channel's medical orders. “They will use the gap,” Talin said. “I know.” “The current actors have not stopped because Corren told the truth.” “I know.” Corren could restore access with one signature. Sere could preserve it by delaying the boundary until after the next request. Either choice would make revelation profitable: learn the breach, keep the benefit, address trust later. Sere surrendered the access cards at the public counter. The clerk asked why three active permissions were being returned at once. Sere wrote sponsoring authority withdrawn by subject. The phrase made autonomy visible and gave the institution a reason to question her judgment. She signed it anyway. Talin returned her observer cord beside the cards. For the first time since the case became dangerous, neither woman possessed a formal route into the evidence pursuing them. Corren took the envelope. The door remained open. She remained outside.
