Chapter 17 Nemi's home court kept no list of the dead on its walls. It kept doors. Old panels stood beneath a shelter beside the salt channel, repaired into tables and screens. Hinge marks, children's heights, smoke stains, and failed paint remained visible beneath new use. The court recognized visitors first through work. Sere carried a repaired axle; Talin carried bread accounts. At each courtyard people touched the cart before adding water or food. The gesture looked ceremonial until Sere saw them checking whether the wheel ran hot. An elder called Nemi by a childhood name as she passed the cart. Nemi answered without turning. “Not that name here.” The elder stopped, apologized, and used Nemi. Nothing in the court rearranged to make the refusal dramatic. A child still demanded the brake cord; someone still shouted that the south kettle boiled dry. The old name remained part of belonging and ceased to be permission for its public use. Sere did not ask what it meant. Nemi gave her the axle pin and pointed toward the wheel. Nemi arrived with the cart and looked first at Sere's hands, then at the axle. “Have you eaten?” Sere said the train had served tea. Nemi broke a heel from the bread Talin carried and put it on the cart rail, where refusing it would require an announcement. Then they shifted a sack toward the left wheel and asked Sere to lift while they listened to the hub. Talin reached for the heavier end. Nemi watched the angle of her wrist and assigned her the brake cord instead. They offered no diagnosis and accepted no thanks. Care appeared as redistributed weight, food within reach, and a task chosen so no one had to name the choosing. “The axle before your questions,” Nemi said. “If we break it for your inquiry, you can explain continuity while we walk home.” One woman denied ever using the cart yet moved every child outside its turning radius before Nemi pulled the handle. Her body retained the arc. No one interpreted it for her. * * * A naming meal marked a young person's decision to take responsibility for an abandoned orchard. Names were spoken, but capability and relationship were enacted through pruning tools, transport, water, and harvest meals. The keeper chose a pruning knife with a chipped back and a sound edge. An uncle offered the polished family blade instead. She refused it because its tang had loosened beneath the wrapping; a beautiful tool that concealed failure was no inheritance she wanted to carry. The argument stayed practical. Who would replace the handle, whose trees would be cut first, and whether responsibility began at the meal or at the first winter pruning mattered more than the object's age. The keeper accepted the defective knife only after the crack was marked openly and the repair assigned to three hands. Sere expected a declaration. Nemi handed the keeper a whetstone and asked when the north rows had last been watered. Life in the court continued through duties that could be refused, corrected, and taken up without becoming lore for Sere's case. The young keeper rejected an inherited tool because its handle was split. An uncle called it three generations of care. An apprentice said displayed tools became lies about labor. The tool went to repair, not reverence. Talin named the risk: a family with many witnesses could make its account appear more continuous than a solitary person's truth. Nemi named cases where the court had done exactly that. “So this is not a cure,” Sere said. “For what?” Nemi asked. She had no answer that did not turn their home into an instrument. An older woman sat beside the cart's former driver and denied knowing him. He poured water to the side her hand reached toward. She found a cracked pin by sound; he steadied the frame before she leaned. They allowed anonymous observation for wheel safety and refused relationship review. Useful relation continued without complete identity judgment. The boundary held. Sere felt envy as attention rather than confession. The court could let a relation remain useful without compelling a complete identity decision. It could also hide cruelty beneath communal certainty. Neither record nor practice was innocent. * * * Under one door table, paired cuts marked a repair taught by one person and demonstrated by another. Names were optional; two witnesses were not. The oldest visible pair predated the Federation office in the region. One pair had been chiseled out after a master used the marks to claim apprentices' work. Later marks were placed by the learner. A second board, stripped of provenance by flood, remained useful for instruction but not dispute. Continuity had degrees. Federation certification crossed a threshold; here an object could retain practical value while losing authority. Across neighboring courts the paired cuts meant teacher and learner, owner and repairer, or simply a repair surviving a season. Similar form did not prove shared origin; it showed communities separating responsibility across people before the Federation called it procedural integrity. Beside an older repair, the carpenter showed Sere a damaged term. Its nearest current rendering was *the forgotten name*, though he warned that *name* carried too much identity and too little relation. “Is it a category?” Sere asked. “It is what one translator wrote after the older word stopped agreeing with itself.” Nemi added, “Useful language. Insufficient language.” Sere recorded the rendering as uncertain local translation and did not promote it into the case classification. The carpenter offered no origin story. Paint layers established only that the damaged wording had been copied after one repair and before another. Nemi refused to let age turn approximation into authority. Sere kept the phrase because it named the gap badly enough to remain open. The pruning tool exposed the difference between their methods. Sere found a replacement pin driven across the grain. Nemi found where sap entered the crack only when the blade was used. Material sequence explained the defect; practice exposed it. They repaired the handle and made paired cuts only after the orchard keeper repeated the repair. Knowledge moved among hands and became harder to erase because responsibility for it was shared. The work resisted elegant division. Sere found the replacement pin driven across the grain; Nemi found the place where sap entered only when the keeper leaned into a living branch. The keeper found a third fault neither had seen: the ferrule twisted under a left-handed grip. They took the tool apart on a door-table. Sere measured the pin. Nemi shaved the damp edge by feel. The keeper rejected their first fit because it would hold for demonstration and fail in an orchard. On the second attempt the blade settled without turning. No single method owned the repair. At the platform Nemi carried two cracked handles for apprentices while Sere carried salt fruit from an aunt who regarded travel as a form of underfeeding. Sere offered to redistribute the load. Nemi gave her the fruit and kept the handles. “You are the one who forgets meals,” they said. The answer placed Sere inside an ordinary judgment rather than outside the court as its examiner. She ate one slice before the train arrived. Salt caught in the split skin of her thumb where the repaired tool had slipped. The older woman called after the former driver to slow before the lower turn. Sere did not ask how she knew. Their refusal of relationship review was part of the truth, not an obstacle between Sere and emotional completion. Before departure, Sere requested a rubbing of the oldest marks. The carpenter refused. “You let me inspect them.” “Inspection leaves with your eyes. A copy leaves without you.” She entered measurements made by sight and marked their uncertainty. From the train, the shelter of doors became dark rectangles, each retaining marks from a house it no longer enclosed. Reuse had not restored those houses. It had given damaged material another duty without forcing it to deny the first. The Federation had not invented recognition. It had formalized some ways people already kept one another legible. The uncertain phrase remained in Sere's notes, untranslated enough to resist becoming an answer.
